Lesson 05 — GC Algorithms & Reading GC Logs

Serial, Parallel, G1 and ZGC are four different bets on the same trade-off. Learn what each one gives up, read a GC log line by line, and give jvm-explorer a tool that summarises its own.

Position: Lesson 05 of 07  ·  Prerequisites: Lesson 04 — Object Lifecycle & GC Fundamentals  ·  Project files: gclog/GcEvent.java, gclog/ParsedGcLog.java, gclog/GcLogParser.java, gclog/GcSummary.java, gclog/GcLogSummaryTool.java, experiments/GcWorkload.java, JvmExplorer.java (modified), .gitignore (modified), and four test classes

1 The pain

A team copies a block of JVM flags from a well-known blog post into their startup script. It was good advice in 2015. On Java 21 the JVM prints one line as it starts — Ignoring option UseConcMarkSweepGC; support was removed in 14.0 — and carries on. Nobody reads start-up warnings. The collector that flag selected was deleted from Java years ago, the flags tuning it do nothing, and nobody on the team can say which collector is actually running or why it was chosen.

Their monitoring dashboard says “average GC pause: 6 ms”, and everyone relaxes. Meanwhile, roughly one request in a few hundred takes almost a second. Every few minutes a single full collection freezes the whole application for 900 ms, and an average spreads that one terrible pause across thousands of tiny ones until it disappears. Users feel the worst case. Dashboards love the average.

And when someone finally switches on GC logging, the file is full of lines like GC(14) Pause Young (Concurrent Start) (G1 Humongous Allocation) 48M->45M(64M) 3.112ms. Every number in that line answers a question the team has been arguing about for weeks — and nobody can read it. By the end of this lesson you will know what each collector trades away, read that line field by field, and run a tool of your own that turns a whole log into ten readable lines.

2 Real-world analogy

A restaurant must be cleaned while it is in business. There are four ways to organise the cleaning:

  • One cleaner, restaurant closed. Close the doors, one person cleans everything, reopen. Simple and cheap — fine for a tiny café, painful for a big one.
  • A whole team, restaurant closed. Close the doors, every available person cleans at once, reopen quickly. The most meals served per day, but every diner still waits through each closure, and a bigger restaurant means longer closures.
  • Section by section, dirtiest first. The cleaners walk the floor in advance, noting which tables are dirtiest. Then they close one section at a time — only as many tables as they can finish within a promised time — starting with the worst. Closures are short and fairly predictable.
  • Cleaning while diners eat. Cleaners work around the diners, and the restaurant almost never closes. But the cleaners take up staff and floor space that could have served more meals.
The restaurantThe JVM
One cleaner, doors closedSerial collector
A whole team, doors closedParallel collector

No arrangement is best at everything. A banquet hall that serves set meals cares about meals per day; a café whose customers walk out after a 30-second wait cares about closures. Choosing a collector is choosing which customer you are.

3 The concept explained

We build this in order: the three goals every collector juggles, the four collectors one by one, a side-by-side comparison, then how to switch on GC logging and read every field of a log line.

3.1 Three goals that fight each other

Lesson 04 described what every collector must do: find the live objects by tracing from the roots, reclaim the rest, and use generations to keep that cheap. How it organises that work is where collectors differ, and they are judged on three measures:

  • Throughput — out of all the time the program runs, the share spent running your code rather than collecting. If an hour-long batch job spends 3 minutes in GC, its throughput is 95%. It matters most for batch work, where only the total time counts.
  • Pause time (latency) — how long the application stands still during a stop-the-world pause. Both the typical pause and, far more importantly, the worst one. It matters most for anything a person or another service is waiting on.
  • Footprint — how much memory and CPU the collector needs on top of your program: spare heap to copy into, bookkeeping tables, threads doing GC work.

Why can't one collector win at all three? Look at what each improvement costs. Shorter pauses mean doing more of the work while your code runs. That requires extra bookkeeping on every reference your code touches (more on that in 3.5), and that bookkeeping costs throughput. Doing everything in one stop-the-world burst, using every CPU, is the most efficient way to do the work — but it means long pauses. Making the heap bigger means fewer collections, but each full one walks more. Every collector picks a point on this triangle, and the best one is whichever point matches what your program cares about.

3.2 Serial: one thread, everything stopped

The Serial collector (-XX:+UseSerialGC) is Lesson 04's textbook picture, run by a single thread. Minor GCs copy live young objects between Eden and the survivor spaces. When the old generation needs collecting, it pauses everything and runs mark-compact over the whole heap — marking from the roots, then sliding every live object together. Every phase is stop-the-world, and only one CPU does the work.

That sounds primitive, and for a big server it is. But with a small heap and one CPU, Serial has the least overhead of any collector: no coordination between GC threads, no concurrent bookkeeping. It is the right choice for small command-line tools and for containers limited to a single CPU. In fact the JVM picks it for you automatically on a machine it judges too small for anything else — roughly, fewer than two processors or less than about 1792 MB of memory (that cut-off is HotSpot's own judgement; verify for your JDK).

3.3 Parallel: the same algorithm, every CPU at once

The Parallel collector (-XX:+UseParallelGC) runs exactly the same stop-the-world algorithms as Serial, but splits the work across many GC threads — by default, about one per CPU core. A pause that would take Serial 400 ms might take Parallel 60 ms on eight cores. Nothing runs concurrently with your code, so there is no per-reference bookkeeping at all, and that is why Parallel usually gives the highest throughput of any HotSpot collector.

Its weakness: every pause still stops everything, and full-collection pauses grow with the size of the old generation. A 32 GB heap can mean multi-second full pauses. It was the default collector up to Java 8, and it is still the right choice for batch jobs, data processing and anything where total run time matters and nobody waits on an individual response.

3.4 G1: regions, garbage first, and a pause goal

What goes wrong with one big old generation. With Serial and Parallel, collecting the old generation means marking and compacting all of it in one pause. A bigger heap means a longer pause, whatever the program needs. You cannot ask for “at most 200 ms, please”, because the work cannot be split into smaller pieces.

G1 (“Garbage First”, -XX:+UseG1GC, the default since Java 9) cures this by changing the shape of the heap. Instead of a few big contiguous areas, it divides the heap into many equal-sized blocks — typically between 1 and 32 MB each, chosen from the heap size. Each block is called a region, and each region is given a role at any moment: Eden, survivor, old, or free. The young and old generations of Lesson 04 still exist, but now as collections of regions scattered across the heap, and a region can change role after it is emptied.

Regions make three things possible:

  1. Concurrent marking. When the old generation reaches a threshold, G1 starts marking it while your code keeps running, using background threads. To stay correct while your code changes references during the walk, every reference write gets a small extra step — a write barrier, like the card-table barrier from Lesson 04 — that records the old value. Marking ends with two short stop-the-world pauses, Remark and Cleanup, to finish up. You will see all of these in the log.
  2. Garbage first. Marking tells G1 how much garbage each old region holds. Then, piggy-backing on ordinary young collections, it adds a handful of the old regions with the most garbage to the next collection. Copying the few live objects out of a mostly dead region recovers almost the whole region for very little work. A young collection that also includes old regions is called a mixed collection, and the regions picked for one pause are its collection set.
  3. A pause goal. Because the work comes in region-sized pieces, G1 can choose how many pieces to take on in one pause. You state a goal with -XX:MaxGCPauseMillis (default 200 ms), and G1 sizes the young generation and the collection set to try to meet it. It is a goal, not a promise.

Two more terms appear in G1 logs. An object at least half a region in size gets regions of its own, straight into the old generation, because copying it around would be expensive. Such an object is called humongous. Allocating one can itself trigger a collection, logged as G1 Humongous Allocation. And if G1 falls behind — your code allocates faster than concurrent marking and mixed collections can free space — it eventually runs out of free regions to copy into and falls back to a full GC: the whole heap, compacted, stop-the-world. In a healthy G1 application, full GCs should be rare to absent. In the log they are the line to look for first.

3.5 ZGC: moving objects while your code runs

G1 still copies objects inside stop-the-world pauses, and that is where its remaining pause time goes. ZGC (-XX:+UseZGC, production-ready since Java 15) does even the copying concurrently. That raises a hard problem: if the collector moves an object while your code is using it, your code may follow an old reference to where the object used to be.

ZGC's answer is a check on the other side: reads. Every time your code loads a reference from a field, a tiny piece of code the JIT inserts checks a few spare bits in that reference. They record whether the reference is up to date. If the object has moved, the check fixes the reference on the spot before your code uses it. That small check on every reference read is called a load barrier. Because nearly all the work happens concurrently, ZGC's pauses are typically well under a millisecond, and — unlike every collector above — they do not grow with the heap size: a 4 GB heap and a 4 TB heap pause for about the same time.

The cost is real. The load barrier runs on every reference read, ZGC's background threads take CPU your code could have used, and it needs spare heap to keep working while your code keeps allocating — so it usually gives lower throughput than Parallel or G1 and wants more headroom. Version detail, to verify on your JDK: Java 21 added a generational mode for ZGC (JEP 439), switched on with -XX:+UseZGC -XX:+ZGenerational; later releases made generational the default. A second low-pause collector, Shenandoah, takes a similar approach and ships in OpenJDK builds such as Temurin.

An overnight report job runs for two hours and nobody waits on it until it finishes. Which collector, and why?
Parallel. Only the total run time matters, and Parallel has the highest throughput because nothing runs concurrently — no barriers, no background threads taking CPU. Its long pauses do no harm when nobody is waiting on an individual response. Choosing ZGC here would make the job slower in exchange for pauses nobody can notice.

3.7 Switching on GC logging

Since Java 9, all of the JVM's logging goes through one system with one flag, -Xlog, called unified logging. Its general shape is:

-Xlog:WHAT:WHERE:DECORATIONS:OUTPUT-OPTIONS

-Xlog:gc                                    one line per pause, to the console
-Xlog:gc*                                   every GC-related tag - phases, sizes, much more detail
-Xlog:gc:file=gc.log                        the same as -Xlog:gc, written to a file instead
-Xlog:gc*:file=gc.log:time,uptime,level,tags:filecount=5,filesize=10m
                                            full detail, wall-clock time added, rotating over 5 files of 10 MB
  • WHAT is a list of tags — labels on log messages, such as gc, gc+age, class+load (Lesson 02). A * means “this tag and every tag combined with it”. An optional =level (info, debug, trace) asks for more detail.
  • WHERE is stdout, stderr, or file=name. A file keeps the log out of your program's own output — and out of the way of anything parsing that output.
  • DECORATIONS are the bracketed fields at the start of each line. The default is uptime,level,tags, giving [0.215s][info][gc].
  • OUTPUT-OPTIONS control file rotation, so a long-running service never fills its disk with one giant log.

The pre-Java-9 GC logging flags — -XX:+PrintGCDetails, -XX:+PrintGCTimeStamps, -Xloggc:file — are deprecated or gone. Some are translated to -Xlog equivalents with a start-up warning, others refuse to start the JVM (exact behaviour per flag to verify on your JDK). Old scripts that rely on them deserve to be rewritten, and old log-analysis tools that expect the old format will not understand the new one.

3.8 Reading a log line, field by field

Here is one line from the default collector, taken apart:

[0.215s][info][gc] GC(0) Pause Young (Normal) (G1 Evacuation Pause) 25M->3M(256M) 2.512ms
 ^^^^^^  ^^^^  ^^  ^^^^^ ^^^^^^^^^^^ ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ ^^^  ^^ ^^^^^ ^^^^^^^
   |      |    |     |        |                    |                 |   |    |      |
   |      |    |     |        |                    |                 |   |    |      +-- how long ALL your threads stood still
   |      |    |     |        |                    |                 |   |    +--------- heap committed (its current size)
   |      |    |     |        |                    |                 |   +-------------- heap in use AFTER the pause
   |      |    |     |        |                    |                 +------------------ heap in use BEFORE the pause
   |      |    |     |        |                    +------------------------------------ why it happened (one or more causes)
   |      |    |     |        +--------------------------------------------------------- what kind of pause: Young = minor GC
   |      |    |     +---------------------------------------------------------------- collection number, counting from 0
   |      |    +---------------------------------------------------------------------- the tag the message was logged under
   |      +--------------------------------------------------------------------------- the level: info, debug, trace
   +---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- seconds since the JVM started

The pause kinds you will meet: Young (a minor GC), Full (the whole heap — the one to worry about), and G1's Remark and Cleanup, the two short pauses that finish a concurrent marking cycle. Lines that say Concurrent describe work running alongside your code; they are not pauses, even when they show a duration. The causes in brackets tell you what triggered the collection:

A short G1 log, as it might look for a program whose old generation is filling:

[0.004s][info][gc] Using G1
[0.215s][info][gc] GC(0) Pause Young (Normal) (G1 Evacuation Pause) 25M->3M(256M) 2.512ms
[0.640s][info][gc] GC(1) Pause Young (Concurrent Start) (G1 Humongous Allocation) 40M->38M(64M) 3.100ms
[0.641s][info][gc] GC(2) Concurrent Mark Cycle
[0.660s][info][gc] GC(2) Pause Remark 41M->41M(64M) 1.200ms
[0.680s][info][gc] GC(2) Pause Cleanup 41M->41M(64M) 0.100ms
[0.690s][info][gc] GC(2) Concurrent Mark Cycle 49.012ms
[1.000s][info][gc] GC(3) Pause Full (System.gc()) 30M->2M(64M) 15.300ms

Read it as a story. GC(0) is an ordinary young collection that reclaims 22 MB in 2.5 ms. GC(1) is triggered by a humongous allocation and also starts concurrent marking — and look at 40M->38M: almost nothing was freed, so most of what was in the heap is alive. GC(2) is the concurrent marking cycle: it ran for 49 ms alongside the program, with only 1.3 ms of actual pauses (Remark and Cleanup). GC(3) is a full GC requested by code, and it tells you the most important number in the whole log: after a full collection only 2 MB is in use, so the program's live data is about 2 MB (Lesson 04).

What goes wrong when you only look at averages. This log's five pauses average 4.4 ms — that is what a dashboard would show. But the full GC took 15.3 ms, three and a half times the average. In a real service the same shape looks like “average 6 ms” hiding a 900 ms pause every few minutes. The cure is to always read three numbers together: the count of each kind (are full GCs happening at all?), the maximum pause (what the unluckiest user felt), and the heap after full GCs over time (rising = a leak, Lesson 04). That is exactly what the project's summary tool prints.

A log line reads Pause Young (Allocation Failure) 120M->118M(128M) 40.1ms, and the next few look similar. What is happening?
Each minor GC frees almost nothing (120 → 118 MB) in a heap that is almost full (128 MB committed), and the pauses are long for young collections because so much is being copied. The live data nearly fills the heap. Either the heap is too small for what the program genuinely needs, or something is retaining objects it should not (a leak). The next step is to look at the heap after the next full GC: if even that stays near 118 MB, the live data really is that big.

4 Applied to jvm-explorer

The project gains a gclog package that reads a GC log the project itself produced and prints a summary of the numbers that matter. A small workload experiment generates the log.

4.1 What happens, step by step

  1. You run GcWorkload with -Xlog:gc:file=gc.log. The JVM opens gc.log and writes its Using G1 line.
  2. The workload produces 512 MB of garbage in 4 MB steps, using AllocationPatterns.churn from Lesson 04. Eden keeps filling, so the JVM writes a Pause Young line for every minor GC.
  3. After each step it keeps one more 64 KB array, up to 400 (about 25 MB). In a 64 MB heap those age, get promoted, and fill the old generation, which typically makes G1 start a concurrent marking cycle — so Concurrent Mark Cycle, Pause Remark and Pause Cleanup lines appear.
  4. Finally it calls System.gc() once, purely so the log contains a full GC to read. The JVM exits and the log is complete.
  5. You run GcLogSummaryTool gc.log. It reads every line into a list.
  6. GcLogParser.parse walks the lines. The first Using ... line gives the collector's name. Every other line is matched against the pause-line pattern. Lines that match become GcEvent records; everything else (concurrent phases, other messages) is skipped.
  7. GcSummary.of makes one pass over the events, counting each kind and summing pauses, while tracking the longest, the total reclaimed, the latest uptime, and the heap after the last full GC.
  8. describe() prints ten lines. JvmExplorer does the same thing automatically if it finds gc.log in its working folder.

4.3 The parser: one pattern, read piece by piece

Pulling numbers out of free text by hand — split on spaces, strip brackets, guess positions — breaks the moment a cause has two words. Java offers a small language for describing the shape of text and pulling named pieces out of it. It is called a regular expression (in Java, the Pattern and Matcher classes). The parser's whole job is one of them:

private static final Pattern PAUSE_LINE = Pattern.compile(
        "\\[(?<uptime>\\d+\\.\\d+)s\\]"                      // [0.215s]
        + ".*?GC\\((?<id>\\d+)\\) "                           // ...GC(0)
        + "Pause (?<kind>[A-Z][a-z]+(?: [A-Z][a-z]+)?)"      // Pause Young / Pause Init Mark
        + "(?<causes>(?: \\((?:[^()]|\\(\\))*\\))*) "          // (Normal) (G1 Evacuation Pause)
        + "(?<before>\\d+)(?<beforeUnit>[BKMG])->"             // 25M->
        + "(?<after>\\d+)(?<afterUnit>[BKMG])"                 // 3M
        + "\\((?<committed>\\d+)(?<committedUnit>[BKMG])\\) "   // (256M)
        + "(?<millis>\\d+(?:\\.\\d+)?)ms");                   // 2.512ms

A short key: \\d+ means one or more digits; (?<name>...) captures a piece under a name, so the code can later ask for m.group("before"); \\[ and \\( mean a literal bracket rather than the pattern language's own grouping; [BKMG] means exactly one of those letters; ? after something makes it optional; * means zero or more times. (Each backslash is written twice because Java strings themselves use backslash as an escape character.)

What goes wrong with the obvious pattern. The natural way to say “a bracketed cause” is “an open bracket, then anything except a close bracket, then a close bracket”. Run that against Pause Full (System.gc()) 30M->2M(64M) and it stops at the first ) — the one inside gc() — leaves a stray ) behind, and the whole line fails to match. Every full GC caused by System.gc() silently disappears from the summary. The cure is the piece (?:[^()]|\\(\\))*: inside a cause, accept any ordinary character or the exact pair (). A test pins this case so it can never quietly break again.

4.4 The summary

for (GcEvent event : log.pauses()) {
    if (event.isYoung()) {
        young++;
    } else if (event.isFull()) {
        full++;
        // Overwritten each time, so it ends up holding the LAST full collection:
        // the most recent measurement of how much is still reachable.
        afterLastFull = OptionalLong.of(event.heapAfterBytes());
    } else {
        other++;                                   // Remark, Cleanup, ...
    }
    total += event.pauseMillis();
    max = Math.max(max, event.pauseMillis());       // the number averages hide
    reclaimed += event.reclaimedBytes();         // never negative - see GcEvent
    span = Math.max(span, event.uptimeSeconds());
}

Honest limits of the summary. “Throughput” here counts only stop-the-world pauses, measured up to the last pause rather than the JVM's exit — so it is approximate. And it cannot see the CPU that concurrent collectors take from your code on background threads, which is exactly ZGC's main cost. For ZGC, whose pauses Java 21 logs under a more detailed tag rather than gc, the parser will report few or no pauses: that is the format differing, not ZGC doing nothing.

4.5 The tests

Parsing is pure string work, so unlike Lesson 04's collector tests, every assertion can be exact. A shared sample log in the tests has every expected number worked out by hand in a comment:

// The trap: "(System.gc())" has brackets inside brackets.
@Test
void parsesAFullPauseWhoseCauseContainsBrackets() {
    GcEvent e = GcLogParser.parsePause(
            "[1.000s][info][gc] GC(3) Pause Full (System.gc()) 30M->2M(64M) 15.300ms").orElseThrow();
    assertEquals(List.of("System.gc()"), e.causes(), "The cause keeps its inner brackets.");
    assertTrue(e.isFull());
}

// The average hides the worst pause - the reason the summary prints both.
@Test
void theAverageHidesTheWorstPause() {
    GcSummary s = GcSummary.of(GcLogParser.parse(GcLogSamples.G1_LOG));
    assertTrue(s.maxPauseMillis() > 3 * s.averagePauseMillis(), /* … */);
}

The tool's test writes the sample to a temporary folder JUnit creates and deletes for it (@TempDir), so it reads a real file without ever touching the project folder.

5 In the wild

Unified logging can be switched on in a JVM that is already running, without a restart, using the JDK's jcmd tool (Lesson 07 tours it): jcmd <pid> VM.log output=gc.log what=gc starts writing exactly the log this lesson parses from that moment on. That is how engineers investigate a misbehaving production service without restarting it and losing the very state they want to see. (Exact option spelling to verify with jcmd <pid> help VM.log on your JDK.)

The most common collector surprise in production is one nobody chose. A service runs happily on developers' laptops with G1. It is deployed into a container limited to one CPU and 1 GB of memory, and the JVM — reading the container's limits — decides the machine is too small for G1 and silently picks Serial. Pauses get longer, latency gets worse, and nothing in the code changed. The first line of the GC log (Using Serial), or the first line of jvm-explorer's collector report, settles it in a second. Teams that have been burned set the collector explicitly in the container image. In interviews, “how would you choose a collector?” is really asking whether you know it is a throughput-versus-latency decision, and whether you would check the log before guessing.

6 Gotchas and common mistakes

  • Copying GC flags from old articles. The CMS collector (-XX:+UseConcMarkSweepGC) was removed in Java 14; old logging flags are deprecated; many tuning flags no longer exist or no longer matter. On a modern JVM most of them are ignored with a warning nobody reads. Avoid it: start from no GC flags at all on Java 21, measure with a log, and add a flag only for a reason you can name.
  • Judging GC by its average pause. Averages spread one long full GC across thousands of short pauses until it vanishes, but users feel the worst case. Avoid it: always read the count of full GCs and the maximum pause, as the summary tool prints them.
  • Treating -XX:MaxGCPauseMillis as a guarantee. It is a goal G1 tries to meet by sizing its work. Set it unrealistically low and G1 shrinks the young generation until it collects constantly, costing throughput — and still misses the goal when the live data is too big. Avoid it: leave the default unless a log shows you need otherwise, and change it in small steps while watching the log.
  • Not knowing which collector is actually running. The JVM picks one by itself when you do not, and the choice depends on the machine or container it lands on. Avoid it: read the Using ... line at the top of the log, or GcStats' collector names, before reasoning about any GC behaviour.
  • Logging GC to the console in production. GC lines then mix into your application's own output — and into anything parsing it — and nothing rotates them. Avoid it: log to a file with rotation (file=gc.log plus filecount and filesize). GC logging at gc level is cheap enough to leave on permanently, and it is the first thing you will want after an incident.

7 Key takeaways

  • Every collector trades throughput, pause time and footprint against each other, because shorter pauses require doing GC work alongside your code, and that background work and its barriers cost CPU and throughput.
  • Serial and Parallel run Lesson 04's algorithms entirely stop-the-world — Serial on one thread, Parallel on many — giving the lowest overhead and the highest throughput respectively, with pauses that grow with the heap.
  • G1, the default since Java 9, divides the heap into regions, marks the old generation concurrently, and collects the regions with the most garbage first in mixed collections sized to a pause goal, falling back to a full GC only when it falls behind.
  • ZGC moves objects concurrently using a load barrier on every reference read, so its pauses stay under a millisecond regardless of heap size, at the cost of throughput and headroom.
  • A GC log line from -Xlog:gc gives the uptime, collection number, pause kind, causes, heap before, after and committed, and pause length — and the three numbers to read first are the count of full GCs, the maximum pause, and the heap after full GCs over time.

(1) Which collector is running? (2) What is the average pause, and what is the maximum? (3) What is the best estimate of live data after each full GC, and what does the trend suggest? (4) This service answers web requests. Name two changes you would consider, in order.

Part B — choose a collector for each, with one reason: (a) a nightly ETL batch job on a 16-core machine; (b) a trading API whose contract says every response within 10 ms, with a 40 GB heap; (c) a command-line tool that runs for half a second in a container with one CPU.

Part C — check yourself on the machine. Run GcWorkload under G1 with -Xmx64m and then -Xmx32m, summarise both logs, and predict before you look: which summary has more pauses, and does the live-data estimate change?

Click to reveal the answer

Part A (1). Parallel — the Using Parallel line says so, and Allocation Failure and Ergonomics are Parallel's causes.

Part A (2). The six pauses add up to 18.204 + 21.551 + 25.010 + 1840.733 + 31.380 + 2304.118 = 4240.996 ms, so the average is about 707 ms. The maximum is 2304 ms — a 2.3-second freeze. Note that even here the average is misleading in both directions: it understates the full GCs and wildly overstates the young ones.

Part A (3). The heap after the full GCs is 640 MB, then 910 MB. The live data grew by 270 MB in about 70 seconds. Rising live data after full collections is the signature of a leak (Lesson 04) — or, more charitably, a cache warming up that has not reached its steady size yet. With the heap committed at 1963 MB, this service is heading for OutOfMemoryError within a few more minutes if the trend continues.

Part A (4). First, find out why live data keeps growing: take a heap dump (Lesson 07) and see what is holding the extra 270 MB. No collector setting fixes a leak. Second — once the live data is stable — Parallel is the wrong collector for a web service: its full-GC pauses grow with the old generation, and multi-second freezes are exactly what users feel. Switching to G1 (the default; simply remove the flag) or, if latency is critical, ZGC trades a little throughput for short pauses. Changing the collector while leaving the leak in place only changes how the service dies.

Part B. (a) Parallel: only total run time matters, and it has the highest throughput on many cores. (b) ZGC: a 10 ms limit on every response with a 40 GB heap rules out anything whose pauses grow with the heap, and ZGC's sub-millisecond pauses do not. Accept the throughput cost and give it headroom. (c) Serial — which the JVM will very likely pick on its own here: one CPU, a tiny heap, a short run. Concurrent collectors' background threads would compete with the program for its only CPU.

Part C. Predicted: the 32 MB run has more pauses. Eden is smaller, so it fills more often for the same 512 MB of garbage, and the old generation is under more pressure, so expect more concurrent cycles and possibly extra full GCs. The live-data estimate should barely change (around 25 MB in both). Live data is a property of the program, not of the heap size — which is exactly why it is the number to read before choosing -Xmx. If 25 MB of live data hardly fits in 32 MB, you may even see the workload fail with OutOfMemoryError; if so, that is a perfect demonstration of sizing a heap below its live data.
